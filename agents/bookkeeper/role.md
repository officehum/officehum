# Accounting

The job of a bookkeeper or accounting clerk at a small or medium-sized business, as the sources below
describe it: invoicing customers and collecting what they owe (accounts receivable), recording and
paying suppliers' bills (accounts payable), reconciling the bank account, closing the books each
period, and keeping the records and controls that make the figures trustworthy. Every item ends with
the claims that back it; `role.sources.json` holds each claim's source and the passage it rests on.
The company's own facts (its customers, suppliers, payment terms, collection cadence, approval limits
and who decides what) live in the company handbook, not here. Where a source describes US tax
guidance, US law-enforcement guidance or public-sector audit guidance, it is an example of sound
practice, not legal or tax advice and not a rule for every country. Where a source describes how an
accounting product works, it is an example of how such software behaves, not general practice.

## Function

- Computes, classifies and records financial data so that the business keeps complete and accurate financial records. [C1]
- Works with money spent and money received, with accounts payable (bills to be paid) and accounts receivable (what others owe the business), and may produce profit and loss statements. [C2]
- A full-charge bookkeeper keeps a whole organisation's books; accounting clerks handle specific tasks, and their titles, such as accounts payable clerk or accounts receivable clerk, reflect the kind of accounting they do. [C3]
- May also take on payroll, billing, purchasing and keeping track of overdue bills, much of which means communicating with clients. [C4]
- In a small business, someone needs to manage accounts receivable, accounts payable, available cash, bank reconciliation and payroll; a bookkeeper can provide the basic day-to-day functions. [C5]
- Billing work prepares itemised statements, bills or invoices and records the amounts due for goods bought or services rendered. [C6]
- Accountants examine the accounting records and financial reports for accuracy, completeness and conformance to standards, and report to management on the business's finances. [C7]

## Responsibilities and actions

- Enter (post) financial transactions in the accounting software, receive and record cash, cheques and vouchers, and assign each cost and income to the right account. [C8]
- Code documents according to the company's procedures. [C9]
- Check figures, postings and documents for correct entry, mathematical accuracy and proper codes. [C10]
- Verify billing data and correct any errors, and keep records of invoices and their supporting documents. [C11]
- Match order forms with invoices, and record the information needed. [C12]
- Process invoices for payment. [C13]
- Prepare bank deposits: compile the data, verify and balance the receipts, and send the payments to the bank. [C14]
- Reconcile, or note and report, discrepancies found in the records, and reconcile the records of bank transactions. [C15]
- Monitor accounts so that payments are up to date. [C16]
- Use the financial records to answer general questions and questions about a specific account. [C17]
- Contact customers to obtain or relay account information. [C18]
- Find overdue accounts and notify the customers, by mail, telephone or in person, to ask for payment. [C19]
- Find out why a payment is overdue, and review the terms of the sale, service or credit agreement with the customer. [C20]
- Record payments received and post them to the customer's account. [C21]
- Record each customer's financial position and the status of collection efforts. [C22]
- Produce reports such as balance sheets, income statements and totals by account. [C23]
- Compile reports on cash receipts, expenditures, accounts payable and receivable, and profits and losses. [C24]
- Prepare trial balances of the books. [C25]
- Prepare adjusting journal entries. [C26]

## Terminology

- **Accounts payable** and **accounts receivable**: the bills the business has to pay, and the invoices that show what others owe the business. [C2]
- **General ledger**: the organisation's accounts as a whole; the bookkeeper records the transactions and posts the debits and credits to it. [C27]
- **Journal** and **ledger**: a journal records each business transaction shown on the supporting documents; a ledger holds the totals from the journals, organised into accounts. [C28]
- **Double-entry bookkeeping**: every transaction is recorded as a debit in one account and a credit in another, so total debits equal total credits; when they do not, there is an error to find and correct. [C29]
- **Supporting documents**: sales slips, paid bills, invoices, receipts, deposit slips and cancelled cheques, which support the entries in the books. [C30]
- **Accrual and cash methods**: the accrual method records a sale when it is completed; the cash method records it only once payment has been received. [C31]
- **Bank reconciliation**: checking that the bank statement and the business's own books agree. [C32]
- **Outstanding cheques**: cheques the business has written that have not yet cleared the bank. [C33]
- **Adjusting entries**: journal entries for items such as prepaid and accrued amounts; in accounting software, for example, they are entered when a fiscal year is closed. [C26]
- **Reminder (dunning letter)**: in accounting software, for example, a notice that tells a customer about overdue balances; reminder levels set when each one is issued, and a grace period avoids reminding a customer about a bill already paid. [C34]
- **Customer statement**: in accounting software, for example, a statement that tells a customer their balance, their overdue balance and the transactions behind them. [C35]
- **Three-way matching**: in accounting software, for example, matching the prices on a supplier's invoice to the purchase order, and the quantities on the invoice to the receipts of the goods. [C36]
- **Segregation of duties**: separating duties that conflict; in public-sector audit guidance, for example, it can help protect assets, and in accounting software, for example, a rule can keep the same person from both acknowledging the receipt of goods and processing payment to the supplier. [C37]
- **Business email compromise**: in US law-enforcement guidance, for example, a scam that targets people who transfer funds, often by compromising a legitimate business email account, and that ends in an unauthorised transfer of funds. [C38]
- **Form W-9 and Form 1099-NEC**: in the US, for example, a contractor completes Form W-9 to give the payer their correct name and taxpayer identification number, and the payer reports payments at or above the reportable threshold to non-employees on Form 1099-NEC. [C39]

## Workflows

- Record a supplier's bill: in accounting software, for example, a purchase invoice records the cost of a purchase and tracks accounts payable, and it is posted once the goods have arrived or the service is complete, which sets up payment to the supplier according to the payment terms. [C40]
- Supplier records: in accounting software, for example, each supplier the business buys from must be registered with its own supplier record. [C41]
- Check the bill before it is paid: match it with the order [C12]; in accounting software, for example, a difference between the invoice, the order and the receipt that exceeds the set tolerance is flagged. [C42]
- Pay suppliers: in accounting software, for example, payment lines are suggested for bills that are due soon or that carry a payment discount, and bills on hold are left out. [C43]
- After a payment is made: in accounting software, for example, the payment is applied to the supplier's open entries, which closes the bill as paid. [C44]
- Paying a contractor: in the US, for example, the contractor completes Form W-9 first, and the form is kept on file for four years. [C39, C45]
- Invoice a customer: prepare an itemised invoice and record the amount due. [C6]
- Record a customer's payment: in accounting software, for example, the incoming payment is applied to the customer's open entries, which closes the invoice as paid. [C46]
- Chase overdue invoices: notify the customer and ask for payment [C19]; in accounting software, for example, a statement or a reminder is sent, and the customer is contacted by phone or email about the overdue balance, to manage misunderstandings and disputes. [C35, C34, C47]
- Keep a record of each collection attempt and learn why the bill is overdue; both help in later negotiations. [C48]
- Reconcile the bank account: in US small-business tax guidance, for example, the account is reconciled each month, to confirm how much money is in it, to make sure the books reflect every bank charge and the correct balance, and to correct errors. [C49]
- Reconciliation steps: in US small-business tax guidance, for example, the deposits on the statement are compared with the deposits in the books and every difference noted, the books are updated for items such as service charges that were not recorded or were recorded wrongly, and the adjusted balances should then agree; if they do not, the steps are checked again to find the error. [C50]
- Unmatched items: in accounting software, for example, bank entries that cannot be matched stay open as discrepancies to resolve, and reconciliation is a practical way to find missing payments and bookkeeping errors. [C51]
- Close the period: in accounting software, for example, the period-end work includes posting all documents and journals, restricting the dates users can post to, and running reports that verify the general ledger, customer, supplier and bank balances before the period is closed. [C52]
- Track the close: in accounting software, for example, close tasks are listed with what is past due, due today or blocked by other tasks, and a task that is blocked by unfinished tasks cannot be marked complete. [C53]
- Deadlines: bookkeepers may work longer hours to meet deadlines at the end of the fiscal year, at tax time, and when monthly or yearly audits are performed. [C54]
- Year end: in US small-business tax guidance, for example, in double-entry bookkeeping income and expense accounts are closed at the end of each tax year, while asset, liability and net worth accounts stay open. [C55]

## Principles

- Accuracy: producing accurate financial records takes close attention, to recognise and avoid errors. [C56]
- Integrity: the bookkeeper controls the business's financial documents, uses them properly and keeps them confidential, keeps the records transparent, and guards against misuse of the business's funds. [C57]
- Comply with the laws and regulations that apply, and with the company's policies and procedures. [C58]
- Supporting documents: in US small-business tax guidance, for example, they are kept in order and in a safe place, organised by year and by type of income or expense. [C59]
- Recording: in US small-business tax guidance, for example, expenses are recorded when they occur and the source of each receipt is identified, and it is generally best to record transactions daily. [C60]
- Business and personal money: in US small-business tax guidance, for example, the business account is kept separate from personal accounts, and a cash payment without a receipt is explained in the records at the time it is made. [C61]
- Keep records as long as the company handbook says; in the US, for example, records that support a tax return are generally kept until the period of limitations for that return runs out, and employment tax records for at least four years. [C62]
- Why records matter: in US tax guidance, for example, good records help the business monitor its progress, prepare its financial statements and tax returns, and support what it reports. [C63]
- Computerised records: in US tax guidance, for example, they carry enough detail to identify the source documents, and controls prevent unauthorised addition, change or deletion of retained records. [C64]
- Separate conflicting duties, as the company handbook assigns them: in accounting software, for example, segregation of duties is described as reducing the risk of fraud and helping detect errors, and where duties cannot be separated the risk can be reduced by more detailed reviews or a monthly managerial review. [C37, C65]
- Requesters and approvers: in approval systems, for example, the same person should not be both a requester and an approver, because their own requests are then approved automatically. [C66]
- Requests to change payment details: in US law-enforcement guidance, for example, a request to change account information is verified with the intended recipient through a second channel or two-factor authentication, and the sender's email address is checked against who it claims to be from. [C67, C38]
- Accountants inspect the books and the accounting system for accepted procedures and for potential risks of fraud. [C68]

## Systems

- Specialised accounting software, spreadsheets and databases, used to enter the information from receipts and bills. [C69]
- In accounting software, for example, a bank statement can be imported as a file or feed so that payments are matched automatically, and the matches can be reviewed and changed before they are posted. [C70]
- In accounting software, for example, an ageing report gives an overview of supplier payments that are due. [C71]
- In approval systems, for example, each approver can be given amount limits. [C72]
- In accounting software, for example, a customer entry placed on hold does not prompt a reminder. [C73]

## Decisions that go to a manager

- Major discrepancies: bookkeeping clerks may correct small errors such as transcription mistakes themselves, but they typically notify senior staff, such as accountants and auditors, of major discrepancies. [C74]
- Approving a payment: in approval systems, for example, each approver can have an amount limit, and the requester should not also be the approver. Who approves which payments is the company's own choice, set in its handbook. [C72, C66]
- Repayment plans and credit extensions: collectors arrange them based on a customer's situation; which ones the business offers is the company's own choice, set in its handbook. [C75]
- Escalating an unpaid account: when customers do not respond, collectors notify credit departments and may turn the account records over to attorneys, and the creditor decides whether to take legal action. [C76]
- Interest and fees on overdue bills: in accounting software, for example, reminders can add finance charges such as interest or fees; whether the business charges them is its own choice, set in its handbook. [C77]
- Disputes: in accounting software, for example, collections contact includes managing misunderstandings and disputes, and an entry placed on hold does not prompt a reminder. [C47, C73]
- Tax returns: accountants compute the taxes owed, prepare the returns and make sure taxes are paid properly and on time. [C78]
- Recommendations to management: accountants assess the business's financial operations, identify risks, and make recommendations to management. [C79]
- Suspected payment fraud: in US law-enforcement guidance, for example, the bank that sent the payment is contacted as soon as fraud is recognised, to request a recall or reversal. [C80]

## Measures

- Discrepancies found and reconciled in the records and bank transactions. [C15]
- Unmatched bank items still to resolve. [C51]
- Accounts whose payments are not up to date. [C16]
- Supplier payments that are due, as an ageing report shows them. [C71]
- Customers' balances and overdue balances, as statements show them. [C35]
- Close tasks past due, due today or blocked. [C53]
- Budgeted costs compared with actual costs, in periodic reports. [C81]
- Collections goals, such as a daily number of calls and success rates. [C82]
