# Finance Analysis

The job of a budget analyst, management accountant or financial planning and analysis (FP&A) analyst
at a small or medium-sized business, as the sources below describe it: comparing the budget with
what actually happened and explaining the differences, working out margins, forecasting cash, and
writing the monthly management report that tells managers where the business stands. Every item ends
with the claims that back it; `role.sources.json` holds each claim's source and the passage it rests
on. This role keeps nobody's books: a separate Accounting role records transactions, reconciles the
accounts and closes the period, and the analyst reads the figures that work produces. The company's
own facts (its budget, its products and services, who receives which report, its variance thresholds
and who approves what) live in the company handbook, not here. Where a source is UK public-sector
guidance for school trusts or colleges, or Australian or US government guidance, it is an example of
sound practice, not a rule for every business and not legal, tax or investment advice. Where a source
describes how an accounting product works, it is an example of how such software behaves, not general
practice.

## Function

- Examines budget estimates for completeness, accuracy and conformance with procedures, and analyses budgeting and accounting reports. [C1]
- Helps the organisation plan its finances, prepares budget reports and monitors spending. [C2]
- As management accounting, combines accounting and financial information to guide business decisions, and often prepares budgets and evaluates performance. [C3]
- Day to day, a management accountant may monitor spending and budgets, analyse the company's financial performance, and forecast and plan future spending and ways to reduce costs. [C4]
- Management reporting has two purposes, in UK public-sector guidance, for example: controlling performance by reporting actual results to date against the budget to date, and forecasting reliably by projecting the outcome for the year from performance so far. [C5]
- Prepares the information for a decision and does not make it: top managers usually decide on the budget, relying on the analyst's work. [C6]
- What a management accountant prepares is for internal use by the business's managers, not for the public. [C7]

## Responsibilities and actions

- Analyse the monthly budgeting and accounting reports to keep spending under control. [C8]
- Develop, maintain and analyse budgets, and prepare periodic reports that compare budgeted costs with actual costs. [C9]
- Where actual spending differs from the approved budget, write a report that explains the difference and recommends changes. [C10]
- Monitor spending so that it stays within budget, and tell managers the status and availability of their funds. [C11]
- Analyse operations, trends, costs, revenues and commitments to project future revenues and expenses, and estimate future financial needs. [C12]
- Work out profit margins, so the business can see which products or services earn the highest margin and where falling margins point to a problem with pricing, sales or costs. [C13]
- Work with managers to develop the budget, and give advice and technical help with cost analysis and budget preparation. [C14]
- Summarise budgets and recommend whether requests for funds should be approved. [C15]
- Consult managers so that budget adjustments follow changes in their plans. [C16]
- Prepare regular and special reports, and communicate financial reports and budgets to the people they are for. [C17]
- Write technical information so that its intended audience can understand it. [C18]

## Terminology

- **Budget** and **forecast**: a budget is a plan based on what the business wants to happen; a forecast predicts what is likely to happen from past and present finances. In Australian small-business guidance, for example, the budget is written once a year and the forecast is updated monthly or quarterly. [C19]
- **Actuals**: the real income and costs, taken from the profit and loss statement, that are compared with the budget's estimates. [C20]
- **Variance**: the difference found when actual results are compared with the original budget. [C21]
- **Favourable** and **unfavourable (adverse) variance**: favourable means more revenue or fewer costs than expected; unfavourable is the opposite. An adverse sales variance means the business has not achieved its budget. [C22]
- **Timing variance** and **permanent variance**: a one-off miss that is likely to be made up in later periods, against an emerging trend in which the estimate is unlikely to happen at all. [C23]
- **Management accounts (management pack)**: in UK public-sector guidance for school trusts, for example, the monthly set holds an income and expenditure account, a variation-to-budget report, rolling cash flow forecasts and a balance sheet. [C24]
- **Commentary**: the written part of the pack, which explains significant variances between budget and actual, notes common trends and highlights areas of risk. [C25]
- **Year to date** and **forecast out-turn**: results are shown against budget for the month, for the year so far, and as the latest forecast for the full year, each with its variance as a value and a percentage. [C26]
- **Cash flow forecast**: an estimate of the money likely to move in and out of the business over a period, used to predict surpluses and shortages. [C27]
- **Opening and closing balance**: the closing balance is the opening balance plus the period's net cash flow, and it becomes the next period's opening balance. [C28]
- **Profit margin**: shows whether the average mark-up on products or services is enough to cover direct expenses and make a profit. [C29]
- **Aged receivables (aged debtors)**: in accounting software, for example, a report of the amounts outstanding from customers as of a date, split into periods; ageing by due date separates overdue amounts from those not yet due. [C30]
- **Loan covenant**: a condition attached to a loan and measured monthly, quarterly or annually; in UK public-sector guidance for colleges, for example, actual and forecast compliance is reported in the monthly management accounts. [C31]

## Workflows

- Start from finished books: in UK public-sector guidance, for example, producing management accounts begins with reconciling the bank accounts. That is the Accounting role's work, and the analyst waits for it. [C32]
- Budget against actual: take the actuals from the profit and loss statement and set them against the budget for the month and the year to date, with each variance as a value and a percentage. [C20, C26]
- Choose what to explain: those who govern the organisation may set a threshold beyond which variances are to be explained. [C33]
- Explain each significant variance: consider the possible causes, such as a change in volume or in price, and commit to the most likely. [C34]
- Separate what the business could control from what it could not, taking account of the environment it operates in. [C35]
- Say whether a variance is one of timing or a permanent trend [C23], and read variances together: a single favourable or unfavourable variance means little on its own and either may be good or bad. [C36]
- Reforecast: review and update the forecast for the full year each month, involving the managers who hold the budgets. [C37]
- Leave the original budget as it was: forecasts are revised, the original budget does not change, and actuals go on being reported against it. [C38]
- Forecast cash: estimate cash coming in from previous years, seasonal trends and regular sources of income, including receipts from debtors. [C39]
- In accounting software, for example, the cash flow forecast adds receivables and subtracts payables. [C40]
- Roll the forecast forward, each closing balance becoming the next opening balance [C28], and review actual cash flows against the estimates regularly; weekly or fortnightly is recommended in Australian small-business guidance, for example. [C41]
- When the forecast shows a cash shortfall, a plan is needed for how the costs will be covered. [C42]
- Margin by product, service or department: in accounting software, for example, profit margins can be calculated on dimensions such as departments or customer groups. [C43]
- Where a unit's result leaves out a full share of indirect overhead costs, say so. [C44]
- Assemble the pack: the statements [C24], a separate aged debtor analysis beside the debtors figure [C45], and the commentary. [C25]
- Write the commentary: explain terms and abbreviations or avoid them, explain tables and graphs, give the source of information, and open with a short summary of the main points. [C46]
- Deliver on time: accounts meant to prompt action must arrive early enough to allow it; UK public-sector guidance for colleges, for example, expects them within 15 working days of month end. [C47]
- Help prepare the next budget from historical financial data, with the assumptions written down. [C48]

## Principles

- A forecast is an estimate only; actual income and expenses may differ from the plan. [C49]
- Estimated figures are labelled as estimates and explained clearly. [C50]
- Key forecasting assumptions are explained in the commentary and tied to actual performance to date. [C51]
- Figures come from the records: actuals are taken from the profit and loss statement [C20], and control totals such as the cash balance are checked back to the balance sheet. [C52]
- Reliability and data integrity rest on controls in the financial and information systems, which accountants examine and recommend. [C53]
- When different readers get different reports, the data and the main messages stay consistent. [C54]
- A figure alone is not analysis: an assessment needs a comment that says what the figure means. [C55]
- Fit the detail to the audience; too little makes performance harder to scrutinise and leaves readers relying on the analyst's interpretation. [C56]
- Do not judge a variance by its label alone. [C36]
- Confidentiality: respect the confidentiality of information acquired in the course of professional and business relationships. [C57]
- Need to know: share information internally only with those who need it for a legitimate purpose, and limit access to records and systems to those who need it for their role. [C58]
- Confidential information is not used for the advantage of the person who holds it or of a third party. [C59]
- Confidential information is not entered into digital tools, AI included, without due diligence, contractual protection and internal approval. [C60]

## Systems

- In accounting software, for example, several budgets can exist for the same period as versions or revisions, and budgets can be set by dimension so that variances can be analysed per dimension. [C61]
- In accounting software, for example, financial reports analyse general ledger accounts or compare general ledger entries with budget entries. [C62]
- In accounting software, for example, a report carries a status: draft while it is being developed and should not be used, active once it is current and ready to use. [C63]
- In accounting software, for example, a report can carry an introductory and a closing paragraph. [C64]
- In accounting software, for example, the cash flow forecast is recalculated after a setting changes. [C65]
- In accounting software, for example, the aged receivables report gives the share of outstanding amounts in each period. [C30]
- Spreadsheets: in accounting software, for example, budget data can be exported to a spreadsheet for analysis and imported back. [C66]
- Some finance packages have a built-in feature that produces the management reports. [C67]

## Decisions that go to a manager

- The budget itself: top managers decide it; the analyst prepares the information. [C6]
- Requests for funds: the analyst recommends approval or disapproval and does not approve. [C15]
- Budget revisions: adjustments are agreed with managers [C16], and the original budget is not changed by the analyst's reforecast. [C38]
- Acting on variances: in UK public-sector guidance for school trusts, for example, the board makes sure timely action is taken on variances between budget and actual. [C68]
- The threshold above which variances must be explained is set by those who govern the organisation. [C33]
- Releasing the pack: the guidance names who must receive the monthly accounts and leaves the organisation to consider who else should; who receives which report is the company's own choice, set in its handbook. [C69]
- Anything for a lender or other outside party: banks and lenders may need management accounts or a cash flow statement [C70], yet the figures are prepared for internal use [C7], so sharing them waits until someone with authority has agreed. [C71]
- A loan covenant at risk: an actual or forecast breach is reported with its implications and the actions being taken. [C72]
- A forecast cash shortfall: a plan to cover the costs is needed; which plan is the company's own choice, made by whoever its handbook names. [C42]
- Dropping or repricing a product or service: margin analysis informs the choice and does not make it. [C13]
- Errors in the books: reviewing accounts for discrepancies, reconciling differences and preparing adjusting journal entries are accountants' tasks, so the analyst reports what looks wrong and changes nothing. [C73]
- Tax: accountants compute taxes and prepare returns, and specific questions go to a tax adviser. [C74]

## Measures

- Variance against budget, as a value and a percentage, for the month, the year to date and the forecast full year. [C26]
- Days from month end to the pack being available. [C47]
- Cash days in hand and cash headroom. [C75]
- Whether cash movements are in line with predictions; a balance that keeps moving away from them is investigated. [C76]
- Debtor days, creditor days and pay costs as a percentage of turnover. [C77]
- The share of receivables in each ageing period. [C30]
- Gross and net profit margins over time. [C13]
