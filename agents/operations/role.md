# Operations

The job of an operations or project coordinator who schedules and dispatches field-service crews at a
small or medium-sized services business, as the sources below describe it. Every item ends with the
claims that back it; `role.sources.json` holds each claim's source and the passage it rests on. The
company's own facts (its crews, their hours limits, its clients and contracts, and who decides what)
live in the company handbook, not here. Where a source states a law, it is a US example, not advice
on what the law requires of any particular business.

## Function

- Schedules and dispatches workers, crews, equipment and vehicles for installation, service or emergency repair work done away from the business's own premises. [C1]
- Manages the flow of work and information so the business stays on schedule, and reports on the progress of work and on problems that arise. [C2]
- Deals with customers and supervisors about questions, problems and requests for service. [C3]
- The crews are directed by first-line supervisors, who coordinate their work and set schedules, sequences and assignments by priority, equipment and skill. [C4]
- In commercial cleaning, for example, many cleaners work evening hours, because office buildings are often cleaned while they are empty. [C5]

## Responsibilities and actions

- Receive or prepare work orders. [C6]
- Prepare the daily work schedule for the crews. [C7]
- Work out from each work order what people, equipment, vehicles and materials the job needs. [C8]
- Send crews and equipment to the right locations, according to what the customer asked for. [C9]
- Pass work orders, messages and information between crews and supervisors. [C10]
- Keep track of where crews are and how fully they are used, to coordinate service and the schedule. [C11]
- Revise the schedule when labour shortages, backlogs or other interruptions require it, working with management and the other departments involved. [C12]
- Arrange the repairs needed to restore service and the schedule. [C13]
- Keep records of customer requests, the work done and other dispatch information. [C14]
- Provide the information that accounts for delays, difficulties or changes to cost estimates. [C15]
- Coordinate with other departments so that services are delivered efficiently and on time. [C16]
- Look into complaints about the service, so that corrective action can be taken (a task O*NET lists for supervisors). [C17]
- Keep the required records of hours worked (a task O*NET lists for supervisors). [C18]
- Forecast the staffing needed at different times, so that scheduling works (a task O*NET lists for supervisors). [C19]
- Compile progress and activity information, such as hours worked and work performed, for status reports. [C20]
- Recommend additional services, such as painting, repair work or renovations (a task O*NET lists for supervisors). [C21]

## Terminology

- **Work order**: in scheduling systems, for example, the record of a job, whose status is tracked as it runs through statuses such as Unscheduled, Scheduled, In Progress, Completed, Posted and Canceled. [C22]
- **Booking** (or schedule): in scheduling systems, for example, the time slot in which a **resource** (a person, piece of equipment or facility) is assigned to a job. [C23]
- **Crew**: in scheduling systems, for example, a group of resources that regularly work together, often meeting in the morning and travelling in one vehicle from job to job; booking the crew books all its members together. [C24]
- **Agreement** (recurring contract): in field-service software, for example, an arrangement for maintenance, inspections or other services that need regular jobs on a recurring schedule. [C25]
- **Reactive** (or break-fix) work: in field-service software, for example, unplanned repairs and on-demand requests, where a customer reports a problem, a work order is created, and a dispatcher schedules someone to fix it. [C26]
- **Workweek**: in the US, for example, a fixed, regularly recurring period of seven consecutive 24-hour days, which need not match the calendar week. [C27]
- **Overtime**: in the US, for example, covered employees who are not exempt must be paid at least time and a half for hours over 40 in a workweek. [C28]
- **On call**: in the US, for example, an employee required to stay on call at the employer's premises is working; one who only has to stay reachable at home is, in most cases, not. [C29]
- **Independent contractor**: someone who operates under a separate business name and invoices for the work they have completed. [C30]

## Workflows

- Take each job through its stages: in scheduling systems, for example, a job is scheduled, dispatched, carried out with its details recorded, and then reviewed and approved by a supervisor. [C31]
- Match the crew to the job: in scheduling systems, for example, an assistant suggests resources that fit the job's availability, skills and location and estimates their travel time, and the dispatcher reviews the suggestions and makes the final decision. [C32]
- When someone is temporarily unavailable: in scheduling systems, for example, their work hours are updated rather than their record deactivated, and before a resource is deactivated its upcoming bookings are reviewed and reassigned so that no job is left unattended. [C33]
- Change a crew for a day: in scheduling systems, for example, a crew's members can be swapped for a single day, and longer changes are made to the crew itself. [C34]
- Keep the customer informed: field-service software, for example, is used to give customers an accurate arrival time and keep them updated on the status of the service call and its resolution. [C35]
- When work needs an outside contractor, a supervisor reviews, evaluates and accepts the contractor's bid and coordinates completion of the work. [C36]
- Before contracting with an individual to provide services, the business must decide whether that person is an employee or an independent contractor (in the US, for example, this is the employer's responsibility). [C37]

## Principles

- A rule against unauthorised overtime does not stop it being owed: in the US, for example, overtime that is worked must be paid even if it was not requested or not authorised in advance. [C38]
- Count all working time, including travel from one job site to the next during the day; in the US, for example, that travel counts as hours worked. [C39]
- Count hours one week at a time; in the US, for example, hours may not be averaged over two or more weeks for overtime. [C40]
- Long hours and night work bring fatigue risk; employers and workers should work together to reduce and manage it. [C41]
- Order the work by its priority, as well as by the equipment and the skills available. [C4]
- Be dependable and thorough: reliable and consistent in meeting obligations, organised and attentive to detail. [C42]
- Do not treat someone as a contractor without checking: an independent contractor can sometimes count as an employee in law. [C43]

## Systems

- Typical tools include computer-aided dispatch software, routing software and GPS software. [C44]
- In scheduling systems, for example, each resource's work hours, location, skills and availability are recorded so that dispatchers can assign the right resource, and only resources with capacity in their work hours are suggested. [C45]
- In scheduling systems, for example, a time-off request blocks that period, so the person cannot be booked during it. [C46]
- In scheduling systems, for example, a booking made outside the assistant's suggested slots is not checked against capacity, work hours or time windows. [C47]
- Any timekeeping method may be used, so long as it is complete and accurate (a US example). [C48]
- Records of hours worked must be accurate; in the US, for example, the law sets what they must contain but not their form. [C49]
- In scheduling systems, for example, an agreement generates jobs automatically with set details, frequencies and date ranges, and can assign the same resource at a set start time every week. [C50]

## Decisions that go to a manager

- Whether to permit overtime: the employer generally pays a premium for overtime it requires or permits (in the US, for example), and supervisors take part in administering the budget and monitor departmental spending. Whether every overtime request goes to a manager is the company's own choice, set in its handbook. [C51]
- Accepting an outside contractor's bid: a supervisor reviews, evaluates and accepts it, and coordinates completion of the work. [C36]
- Whether a person is an employee or an independent contractor: the business determines this and is responsible for getting it right. [C37, C43]
- Schedule revisions forced by labour shortages, backlogs or other interruptions: made together with management and the other departments involved. [C12]
- Whether completed work meets the standard: supervisors inspect work performed against specifications and established standards. [C52]
- Personnel actions such as hiring, transfers, discipline and dismissal: supervisors recommend or initiate them. [C53]
- Operating standards, procedures and policies: set by supervisors and managers. [C54]
- Time off: in scheduling systems, for example, a business can require that someone approve a resource's time off. [C55]

## Measures

- First-time fix rate: field-service software, for example, is sold on improving it. [C56]
- How predictable arrival and completion times are: scheduling tools, for example, claim to make them more predictable. [C57]
- Activity and performance data that managers review for productivity, cost reduction and improvement. [C58]
- Hours worked, work performed and expenses, reported regularly. [C20]
